# Offers lens
Score: 5 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong

1. **The pain is real and specific.** Church organizers juggling WhatsApp, spreadsheets, and manual Pix verification is a genuine daily headache. The dream outcome — "one place for registrations, payments, and check-in with zero spreadsheet wrangling" — is easy to articulate and easy for the buyer to picture.

2. **BYOG reduces effort and sacrifice on the trust axis.** In a market where financial transparency is non-negotiable, never touching the organizer's money removes a massive objection. This is not a feature — it is a structural trust guarantee, and it lowers the perceived risk of adopting the platform to near zero.

3. **The free plan lowers time-to-value to minutes.** A buyer can run a real event on Chinám before spending anything. That collapses the "time delay" and "effort and sacrifice" sides of the value equation. Most SaaS in this niche gates functionality behind a paywall; a working free tier is a genuine differentiator for non-technical, budget-conscious buyers.

4. **Cost to serve is tiny.** At R$4–10 per customer per month, gross margins on a R$119/month plan are enormous. The business does not need scale to be profitable — it needs a handful of paying customers to break even, which means the offer can be generous.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)

1. **No proof, no likelihood of achievement.** The value equation's "perceived likelihood of getting the result" is currently zero for any prospect. There are no case studies, no testimonials, no public event count, no "X registrations processed" badge. A church leader choosing software for their community will ask "who else uses this?" and hear silence. Without social proof, even a perfect offer gets ignored — the buyer cannot believe it works.

2. **The market has money, but willingness to pay for software is unproven.** Churches and community groups in Brazil operate on tight, donation-funded budgets. They have *some* money (they already spend on venues, food, printed materials), but whether they will allocate R$119/month to event software — when the current WhatsApp-and-spreadsheet workflow is free — is an open question. The offer has not been tested at price. If the answer is "they will only use the free plan," the business has no revenue regardless of how good the offer is.

3. **No guarantee and no stack.** The current offer is a feature list: registrations, payments, check-in. Through the Offers lens, a strong offer stacks solutions to every problem the buyer hits on the way to the outcome, then wraps it with a guarantee. Tovia has neither. There is no "run your first event in 48 hours or we set it up for you." There is no onboarding concierge, no event-planning template pack, no post-event analytics report bundled in. The offer is functional but flat — it competes on being "the tool," not on being "the obvious choice."

## The questions I would ask the founder

1. Have you tested willingness to pay at R$119/month with even five real prospects — not friends, not hypotheticals — and what did they say?
2. What happens when a prospect asks "who else uses this?" What proof can you show them today?
3. Could you double the price of Chalém to R$598/month and still be the obvious choice? If not, what would you need to add to justify it?
4. What is every problem an organizer hits between "I want to run a retreat" and "the retreat is done and the finances are closed" — and which of those problems does Tovia not solve today?
5. What would you guarantee? If a church runs an event on Tovia and it goes badly, what do you promise?
6. What is your customer acquisition cost (CAC) going to be? How do you reach the first 50 paying churches, and what does each one cost you to acquire?

## If FUND IF: the conditions

1. **Get five paying customers at the published price within 90 days.** Not free-plan users — paying Pétach or Chalém subscribers who renew at least once. This proves willingness to pay and gives the business its first proof.
2. **Build and publish social proof before scaling.** At least three case studies or public testimonials from real events, with numbers (registrations processed, payments collected, time saved). The offer cannot grow without proof.
3. **Stack the offer beyond features.** Add at least two high-value, low-cost deliverables — onboarding setup assistance, event-planning templates, a post-event financial summary — so the paid plans feel like an unfair deal, not just "more features."
4. **Register the CNPJ.** A business selling to churches cannot invoice informally. This is table stakes for trust in this market.
