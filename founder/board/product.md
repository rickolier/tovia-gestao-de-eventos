# Product lens
Score: 6 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong

The core insight is sound: church event organizers in Brazil cobble together WhatsApp, spreadsheets, and manual Pix verification. That workflow has real seams — missed payments, no check-in record, no financial summary — and Tovia collapses it into one flow. The BYOG model is not just a cost decision; it is a product decision. It removes the single moment that would make a church treasurer hesitate ("where does the money go?"). That shows genuine understanding of the customer's emotional reality, not just their functional needs.

The free plan (Chinám) that actually works is the right call. In a trust-sensitive, non-technical audience, a crippled trial creates suspicion. A working free tier lets organizers run one small event, see it work, and upgrade because they want more — not because they were forced. That is how the first five minutes should feel: "I just ran my event without a spreadsheet."

Low infrastructure cost means the product can survive a slow start without outside money. That is an underrated product advantage — it buys time to iterate on craft.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)

1. **The experience between systems is undesigned.** Tovia handles registration, payment, and check-in — but the organizer's life does not start or end there. They announce in WhatsApp, they follow up in WhatsApp, they report to church leadership in a meeting. Every handoff — exporting a list, sharing a payment link in a group, generating a report for the pastor — is a seam where the experience breaks. If Tovia does not own those transitions (a shareable link that looks right in WhatsApp, a one-page PDF report for leadership, a message template the organizer copies), it will feel like yet another tool bolted onto their existing chaos instead of replacing it.

2. **Simplicity has not been stress-tested with real users.** The brief says "simple interface, no technical jargon," but zero paying customers means zero evidence that this is true for actual pastors and church secretaries. Non-technical users do not complain about complexity — they just stop using the product. Without five real organizers running real events, "simple" is an aspiration, not a fact. The danger is that the founder, who built it, confuses his own fluency with the product for the customer's.

3. **Too many features, not enough identity.** Registration, payments, QR check-in, event finances — that is four products in a trench coat. The product lens asks: what is the one thing? If forced to pick, is Tovia the best way to collect event payments transparently, or the best check-in tool, or the best registration form? Trying to be all four equally means none of them will be remarkable enough to spread by word of mouth. Churches adopt tools through pastor-to-pastor recommendation; that only happens when someone says "you have to see this one thing."

## The questions I would ask the founder

- Walk me through the last event you tested this on, minute by minute, from the moment the organizer opened Tovia to the moment the event was over. Where did they get stuck? Where did they leave Tovia and go to WhatsApp or a spreadsheet?
- What happens when an attendee pays via Pix but the gateway confirmation is delayed? What does the organizer see? What does the attendee see? (This is the seam most likely to create distrust.)
- If you could only keep one feature — registration, payment, or check-in — which one would you keep, and why?
- Show me the screen an organizer sees after their event ends. Is there a "here is what happened" summary they can screenshot and send to their pastor? If not, why not?
- What does your product look like in a WhatsApp group? (This is where 90% of your distribution will happen — links shared in groups. If the preview card is ugly or confusing, the product is invisible.)

## If FUND IF: the conditions

1. **Five real events run on the platform before any growth spending.** Not demos, not the founder's test data — five actual church events with real attendees and real money collected through BYOG. Document every friction point. This is the only way to know whether "simple" is real.
2. **Define the one thing.** Pick the single capability that will make an organizer tell another organizer about Tovia, and make that capability flawless. Everything else can be good enough; one thing must be undeniable.
3. **Design the WhatsApp touchpoints explicitly.** The shareable registration link, the payment confirmation the attendee can forward, the post-event summary — these are not features; they are the product as the customer actually encounters it. If these feel clumsy or generic, the core app does not matter.
