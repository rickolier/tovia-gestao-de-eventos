# Monopoly lens
Score: 4 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong

Tovia starts with a small, well-defined market — church and community event organizers in Brazil — and has a realistic shot at owning that niche before generalist platforms notice it. The BYOG (Bring Your Own Gateway) model is a genuine insight: it sidesteps the trust problem that keeps churches away from platforms that intermediate money. That is not a 10x technological breakthrough, but it is a 10x trust improvement for this specific buyer, and trust is what matters in this market. The cost structure (serverless, near-zero marginal cost per customer) means the business can reach profitability at a tiny scale, which gives it time to build defensibility before needing outside capital.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)

1. **No moat after product-market fit.** BYOG is a design decision, not proprietary technology. Any developer can replicate it in weeks. There are no network effects (each church operates independently), no meaningful economies of scale at this stage, and no brand yet. If Tovia proves the niche works, Sympla or a well-funded competitor can copy the playbook with more resources and an existing user base. The business needs to build a defensibility plan — community, data, integrations, switching costs — before it proves the market to outsiders.

2. **Distribution is undefined.** The brief says zero paying customers and gives no distribution plan. Pastors and church leaders are reachable (WhatsApp groups, denominational networks, church conferences), but actually converting them requires a specific, repeatable channel. A solo founder with no sales or marketing background faces a real risk of building a good product nobody finds. How do the first 100 customers arrive, and what does each one cost? This is unanswered.

3. **Solo founder, no team.** The Monopoly lens asks if this is the right team. A solo non-technical-business founder (self-taught developer, no management background) can build an MVP but will struggle to simultaneously sell, support, build, and grow. There is no one to hold the product while the founder does distribution, or vice versa. This is a people risk, not a product risk.

## The questions I would ask the founder

1. What is your distribution plan for the first 100 paying customers — specifically, which channels, what is the expected cost per acquisition, and what is the timeline?
2. What stops Sympla, Even3, or a new entrant from adding a BYOG mode and a "churches" vertical in six months once the niche is validated?
3. What is the secret — what do you know about this market that others do not, and that you learned by being in it rather than by researching it?
4. What does the retention curve look like? Once a church runs one event on Tovia, do they come back for the next one, or do they revert to WhatsApp?
5. Is there a network effect you can engineer — for example, attendees of one church's event discovering Tovia and bringing it to their own church?
6. What is the actual TAM (total addressable market) for church event management SaaS in Brazil? How many churches run paid events regularly enough to justify a monthly subscription?

## If FUND IF: the conditions

1. **Distribution proof.** Show 20–30 paying customers acquired through a repeatable channel (not personal network), with a documented CAC and conversion rate, within six months.
2. **Defensibility plan.** Present a concrete strategy for building at least one durable advantage — switching costs through integrations (church management systems, financial reporting), community/network effects, or data that improves the product over time.
3. **Team.** Bring in at least one co-founder or key hire who covers the founder's blind spot (business development, sales, or growth). A solo founder is acceptable for an MVP; it is not acceptable for a go-to-market push.
4. **CNPJ and legal entity.** Formalize the business so it can invoice, sign contracts, and operate as a real vendor to institutional buyers (churches often have formal purchasing processes).
